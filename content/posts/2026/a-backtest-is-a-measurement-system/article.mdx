An equity curve is the last stage of a chain of assumptions. It is produced only after an author chooses a universe, a timestamp convention, a data vendor, a signal, a portfolio construction rule, a rebalancing schedule, transaction-cost model, benchmark, and selection procedure. A backtest is therefore best treated as a measurement system. Its result is conditional on a protocol, not a forecast of future returns.

The figures and values in this note are synthetic. They exist to exercise the reporting components and to show the difference between a legible research record and a persuasive-looking chart. This is not investment research or an investment recommendation.

<Callout type="caution">
  Every number, strategy name, return path, and ranking below is a fabricated fixture. Real research must document data licences, corporate-action treatment, timestamp availability, capacity, costs, and the multiple-testing process that led to the rule.
</Callout>

The main discipline is to keep the observation process separate from the trading rule. A factor return, for example, is a historical construction with specified portfolios and definitions; it is not a free variable that can be silently re-created until it confirms a preferred story.<Citation refId="fama-french-1993" label="[1]" /> Data libraries are valuable partly because their definitions and historical revisions can be inspected rather than reconstructed from memory.<Citation refId="ken-french-library" label="[2]" />

## Write a protocol that can fail

<Steps title="Minimum viable backtest protocol">
  <Step title="Specify the data contract">
    Record vendor, universe construction, fields, timezone, corporate actions, point-in-time availability, and missing-data policy.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pre-register the decision rule">
    Fix formation, holding, weighting, limits, rebalance timing, and benchmark before inspecting the final curve.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Simulate implementability">
    Apply delay, spreads, commissions, market impact assumptions, financing, turnover limits, and liquidity filters.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Audit fragility">
    Inspect subperiods, costs, parameter neighborhoods, alternative data treatments, and the full family of attempted variants.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The Sharpe ratio is a useful descriptive summary, but its sampling distribution depends on the return process and the path used to compute it; it should not be read as a self-validating score.<Citation refId="lo-2002" label="[3]" /> If an author tested many related rules before choosing one, the selection process changes the meaning of the selected statistic. Deflated-Sharpe-style adjustments are attempts to make that choice visible, not a licence to search until a threshold is met.<Citation refId="bailey-2014" label="[4]" />

<Tabs caption="Each tab answers a distinct interpretation question; it is not a place to hide inconvenient results.">
  <Tab title="Gross result">
    Gross returns answer a narrow modelling question. They do not describe an executable portfolio.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Net result">
    Net returns incorporate stated trading and financing assumptions. Their credibility depends on whether those assumptions are conservative and testable.

    A sensitivity report shows whether a conclusion changes across plausible delays, costs, universes, and parameter neighbourhoods.

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="Why is a benchmark not enough?" open>
    A benchmark gives context for a realised path. It does not repair data leakage, selection bias, execution assumptions, or an unclear estimand.
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="Why retain failed variants?">
    They reveal the scope of the search and help a reader distinguish a robust mechanism from a fragile selected configuration.
  </AccordionItem>
