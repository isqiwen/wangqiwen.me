MRI reconstruction is often discussed as a competition between algorithms. That is too narrow. An accelerated scan is a measurement system: an acquisition selects samples from k-space, a forward operator maps a proposed image to those measurements, and a reconstruction method contributes assumptions about what images are plausible. A learned model changes the third part; it does not make the first two disappear.

This note is a methodological template rather than a clinical study. All visual panels, tables, numerical values, and classifications below are synthetic rendering fixtures. They demonstrate what a complete technical report must expose; they make no claim about diagnostic performance or patient outcomes.

<Callout type="caution" title="Scope and evidence">
  The illustrations are not patient images, and the tabulated values are not experimental results. A clinical claim requires a protocol, an independent test set, reader study design where appropriate, and a safety analysis matched to the intended use.
</Callout>

## State the inverse problem before selecting an architecture

<Definition id="def-mri-forward-model" label="1" title="Multicoil acquisition model">
  Let <InlineMath tex="x \in \mathbb{C}^N" /> be the target image, <InlineMath tex="S" /> coil sensitivity operators, <InlineMath tex="F" /> the Fourier encoding, and <InlineMath tex="M" /> a sampling mask. The acquired data are <InlineMath tex="y = MFSx + \varepsilon" />, where <InlineMath tex="\varepsilon" /> represents noise and model mismatch.
</Definition>

The point of <CrossReference target="def-mri-forward-model" label="Definition 1" /> is not notation for its own sake. It forces an author to distinguish an image prior from a claim about data consistency. When a reconstruction is evaluated, readers should be able to ask which part of the pipeline changed: the sampling operator, calibration, coil model, regularizer, or solver.

  caption="A generic regularized reconstruction objective. The data-fidelity term alone cannot select one image from an undersampled acquisition."
/>

<DerivationBlock

The objective in <AutoEquationRef target="eq-mri-objective" /> is deliberately generic. Sparse MRI made the relationship between undersampling, sparse representations, and nonlinear reconstruction explicit; modern variational and unrolled approaches retain the same inverse-problem structure while learning parts of the reconstruction map.<Citation refId="lustig-2007" label="[1]" /> <Citation refId="hammernik-2018" label="[2]" />

<TheoremBlock id="prop-identifiability" kind="proposition" label="1" title="Data consistency is necessary but not sufficient">
  If the sampling operator <InlineMath tex="A" /> is non-injective on the admissible image class, there can be distinct images <InlineMath tex="x_1 \ne x_2" /> with <InlineMath tex="Ax_1 = Ax_2" />. A reconstruction rule therefore needs an additional preference, whether handcrafted, learned, or imposed by a low-dimensional parameterisation.
</TheoremBlock>

<ProofBlock title="Why the proposition matters" strategy="null-space argument" conclusion="Validation must test the prior, not merely the residual.">
  When <InlineMath tex="A" /> has a non-trivial null space, choose a non-zero <InlineMath tex="z" /> such that <InlineMath tex="Az=0" />. Then <InlineMath tex="A(x_1+z)=Ax_1" />. Both candidates agree with the acquired samples, so data fidelity cannot distinguish them. A regularizer or learned denoiser selects one candidate by adding information not present in the measurements.
</ProofBlock>

## What the article refuses to claim

<PullQuote author="Methodological principle">
  A low residual is evidence that a candidate image matches the acquired data under a model. It is not evidence that every visible structure is clinically faithful.
</PullQuote>

The most useful model card is therefore not a marketing summary. It records the acquisition domain, exclusions, optimisation objective, reference definition, primary endpoint, known failure modes, and who should decide when the system is uncertain. Public datasets such as fastMRI make reproducible reconstruction benchmarks possible, but a benchmark remains a bounded evaluation setting rather than universal evidence.<Citation refId="fastmri-2019" label="[3]" />

<Tabs caption="Use a tabbed comparison only when the alternatives share the same question.">
  <Tab title="Image fidelity">
    Ask whether the reconstruction matches a defined reference under the stated acquisition conditions. Use metrics with uncertainty and inspect error structure.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Decision fidelity">
    Ask whether a downstream scientific or clinical decision changes. This requires a reference standard and an endpoint defined before analysis.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Operational safety">
    Ask what happens under protocol drift, motion, missing calibration, or an input outside the training distribution.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="Why not report only PSNR or SSIM?" open>
    They are useful image-comparison summaries, but neither metric by itself validates a diagnostic decision, distribution shift, or a clinically material local error.
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="When is a learned reconstruction appropriate?">
    When its training distribution, forward model, validation target, and failure handling are explicit enough for the intended use—not merely when it produces visually pleasing images.
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>
