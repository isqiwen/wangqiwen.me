Historical writing often becomes least reliable at the moment it becomes most readable. A clean quotation, a precise date, or a persuasive narrative can conceal the practical work that made it possible: locating a record, determining what version was seen, preserving reference codes, distinguishing transcription from normalisation, and measuring the distance between an artefact and the claim made from it.

This note proposes a simple rule: provenance is not a footnote after the argument. It is one of the argument's premises. The diagrams and text examples below are synthetic; no real collection, record, or transcription is being represented.

<Callout type="note">
  Primary sources are records from the period under study, but their usefulness depends on questions of creator, purpose, context, preservation, and access. Those questions belong in the method, not merely in a bibliography.<Citation refId="tna-working-records" label="[1]" />
</Callout>

## Treat provenance as a chain, not a label

<Definition id="def-provenance-chain" label="1" title="Chain of custody for a historical assertion">
  A provenance chain is the documented path from a source object through custody, description, digitisation, transcription, and interpretation to a stated claim. Each transformation carries a possible loss, addition, or uncertainty that should remain recoverable.
</Definition>

<Timeline>
  <TimelineItem date="1. Creation" title="An artefact is produced">
    Record the actor, occasion, audience, material form, and purpose if known. These are historical facts to establish, not defaults to assume.
  </TimelineItem>
  <TimelineItem date="2. Custody" title="The artefact enters a collection">
    Preserve repository, collection, series, item identifier, catalogue state, and access date. Precise citations let another reader retrieve the relevant documentary evidence.<Citation refId="tna-citing" label="[2]" />
  </TimelineItem>
  <TimelineItem date="3. Derivative" title="A scan, photograph, OCR, or transcription is made">
    Name the derivative and its procedure. A digitised image is not the physical object; OCR is not a diplomatic transcription.
  </TimelineItem>
  <TimelineItem date="4. Claim" title="An interpretation is published">
    State the inferential step, the scope of the conclusion, and what evidence would weaken it.
  </TimelineItem>
</Timeline>

The insistence on traceability does not make interpretation mechanical. It identifies where interpretation begins. The National Archives' guidance on historical evidence explicitly directs readers to consider perspective, bias, limitations, reliability, and context rather than treating a document as transparent speech from the past.<Citation refId="nara-raw" label="[3]" />

The second column may be appropriate for a general reader, but it has editorial content. Capitalisation, expansions, punctuation, and silently corrected errors are interpretive choices. A formal article should state the transcription policy and preserve a route back to the observed form. Digital history work on transformations makes the same point in computational terms: derivative files and transformations need provenance if later users are to understand what they see.<Citation refId="externalizing-transformations" label="[4]" />

## Use media as evidence only when its record is preserved

<Accordion>
  <AccordionItem title="What is the smallest defensible claim?" open>
    Start with what the object directly records under a stated reading. Move to wider claims only when the inferential bridge, corroboration, and uncertainty are visible.
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="Can a digitised record be cited?">
    Yes, but cite both the archival identity and the digital derivative where possible. A URL alone usually cannot describe the object, version, or relation to a physical source.
  </AccordionItem>
  <AccordionItem title="When should uncertainty appear in the prose?">
    Whenever it changes the scope of the inference: uncertain dating, attribution, transcription, survival, representativeness, or a gap in the chain of custody.
  </AccordionItem>
</Accordion>

## Publish a claim with its limits intact

<Callout type="important">
  A precise citation lets a reader find the artefact. A precise method lets that reader understand the distance between the artefact and the argument.
</Callout>

<Tabs caption="The same artefact can support several forms of writing, provided the transformation is explicit.">
  <Tab title="Catalogue note">
    Prioritise identification: title or supplied title, repository, reference code, date range, material description, and access conditions.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Edition">
    Prioritise transcription policy: witness, normalisation, editorial symbols, variants, and a reliable link from reading text to facsimile.
  </Tab>
  <Tab title="Argument">
    Prioritise inference: which observed feature matters, what alternative explanation exists, and what would change the conclusion.
  </Tab>
</Tabs>
